                                                  CHAPTER I

                                              INTRODUCTION
         Food insecurity remains a critical global and national challenge. According to
DOST-FNRI (2024), food insecurity is intensified by rising food prices, climate-related
agricultural disruptions, and unequal access to social protection, with 44% of households
experiencing moderate to severe food insecurity in 2023. The issue is particularly evident in
Region IV-A (CALABARZON), where around 237,800 residents remain unable to meet basic
food needs and poverty incidence varies significantly across provinces (PSA RSSO IV-A, 2025).
Existing monitoring systems rely on infrequent data collection cycles and lack the ability to
provide timely, spatially detailed, and predictive insights for anticipatory intervention.
         Recent advances in machine learning and natural language processing have shown strong
potential for anticipatory food insecurity monitoring using news-derived signals. Studies by
Balashankar et al. (2023), Ahn et al. (2023), and Van Wanrooij et al. (2024) demonstrated that
NLP and deep learning models can improve food crisis prediction accuracy and detect emerging
risk patterns months ahead of traditional indicators. However, these approaches remain limited in
Filipino-English settings and do not address rural media undercoverage. To address these gaps,
this study will develop aiPHeed, a geospatial food insecurity forecasting system for DA Region
IV-A that integrates zero-shot Filipino-English NLP and ensemble machine learning. The system
will aim to support earlier detection of food stress and improve the timeliness and targeting of
government interventions.

                                                                                                                               6
1.1 Project Context
         Monitoring systems for food insecurity exist at different governance levels, but none

addresses the operational needs of a regional agency like DA Region IV-A. WFP (World Food
Programme)'s HungerMap Live monitors food security in over 95 countries using machine
learning, climate, and economic data (WFP, 2026), but its predictive functions cover only 16
Hunger Hotspot countries, excluding the Philippines. Within the Philippines, the government's
primary food-insecurity and poverty-targeting datasets are updated only once every several
years, so the information available to plan a response is already outdated by the time a shock,
such as a commodity price spike or a crop failure, actually occurs. The 2023 DSWD-FAO (Food
and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations) Digital Mapping System under the
Enhanced Partnership Against Hunger and Poverty program focuses on supply-side market
linkages, not food insecurity risk prediction (DSWD, 2023).

         In the research literature, several studies informed the construction of aiPHeed while
revealing gaps it will resolve. Balashankar et al. (2023) built a news-based early warning system
across 21 countries using 11.2 million articles, but their model depends on English corpora and
IPC (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification) phase labels unavailable in
CALABARZON. Carneiro et al. (2025) used NLP and machine learning on over 4,028 FEWS
NET (Famine Early Warning Systems Network) reports to identify market disturbances as the
strongest predictor of food insecurity, but relied on FEWS NET data, which is absent in all
Philippine regions. Van Wanrooij et al. (2024) applied unsupervised BERTopic modeling on
news data to detect food insecurity risk patterns, but their approach does not handle
Filipino-English data or address geographic media bias where capital-city-concentrated reporting
under-reports food stress in rural provinces. Ahn et al. (2023) demonstrated that ML models

                                                                                                                               7
using news data outperform those using traditional indicators, yet were limited to English data
labeled by IPC. Collectively, no system produces quarterly, province-level food insecurity
forecasts for CALABARZON disaggregated to the municipality level; no NLP study has
addressed a Filipino-English news environment or corrected for rural undercoverage; and no
study has derived forecast labels from PSA food CPI data as a proxy for regions where IPC
classifications are permanently unavailable.

         aiPHeed addresses these gaps through a two-tier architecture combining zero-shot
Filipino-English NLP (Natural Language Processing), unsupervised topic modeling, spatial bias
correction, and ensemble machine learning into a single quarterly forecasting pipeline built for a
Philippine regional context. The name aiPHeed itself follows the "i-" naming convention
common to Philippine government platforms such as iGovPhil and iTax, with "i" replaced by
"ai" to reflect its AI foundation, and "Feed" adapted to "PHeed" for its Philippine,
food-insecurity focus. It processes Filipino-English news without annotated training data,
corrects for rural media undercoverage, derives a validated forecast target from publicly
available CPI (Consumer Price Index) data, and delivers spatially disaggregated quarterly risk
forecasts to DA 4A officers through a geospatial web dashboard. By relying exclusively on
publicly and permanently available data, aiPHeed also addresses the sustainability limitation
common to existing early warning systems that depend on restricted or non-renewable sources.

1.2 Purpose and Description

         The purpose of this research is to build an interactive web-based geospatial system,
aiPHeed, which will utilize zero-shot Filipino-English NLP and a LightGBM (Light Gradient
Boosting Machine) ensemble in conducting forward-looking forecasting of food insecurity risk
in Region IV-A (CALABARZON). The Department of Agriculture Regional Field Office IV-A

                                                                                                                               8
(DA 4A) and local government units in CALABARZON have been relying on the DOST-FNRI
Expanded National Nutrition Survey and Philippine Statistics Authority poverty statistics, which
are typically released only every two to three years. As a result, there exists a critical response
lag where households facing food insecurity arising from commodity price shocks, crop losses,
or employment disruptions in the post-survey period remain uncovered under current targeting
interventions (PSA RSSO IV-A, 2025; DOST-FNRI, 2024a; World Bank, 2020). The proposed
aiPHeed will aim to bridge this gap by offering DA 4A a continuously updated,
evidence-informed forecasting tool that will estimate and rank LGU (Local Government
Unit)-level potential risk of food insecurity on a quarterly basis, intended to inform and support
anticipatory planning and preparedness decisions. As with most modern machine learning
techniques, the model at the core of aiPHeed identifies patterns in a way that is not immediately
visible or easy to explain on its own, often described as a "black box." aiPHeed addresses this by
pairing every forecast with a SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)-based explanation that
shows, in plain terms, which factors most influenced the prediction, so that a non-technical DA
reviewer can understand and trust a result before acting on it.

         In addition, the system will produce risk rankings for all 142 cities and municipalities in
CALABARZON, incorporating a bias-corrected Food Stress Sentiment Index, trigger
composition scores, temporal momentum features, and PSA food price stress indicators as
reflected in newly released government data. The risk rankings produced will be indicative and
not absolute, as they will be relative ordinal estimates of potential risk and not definitive
determinations of actual food insecurity status. Most significantly, aiPHeed applies zero-shot
Filipino-English transfer learning to a news corpus and integrates this with a composite food
stress label combining SWS (Social Weather Stations) quarterly hunger data and PSA

                                                                                                                               9
province-level food CPI deviation, anchored to the FAO SDG 2.1.2 baseline established by the
DOST-FNRI ENNS, and provides results through a publicly accessible geospatial interface with
SHAP-attributed forecast reports. aiPHeed is designed to be a proactive, interpretable, and
scalable system to identify LGUs where the risk of food insecurity is most likely to be rising,
thereby contributing to the achievement of SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 2 (Zero Hunger).

1.3 Statement of the Problem
         CALABARZON's food insecurity monitoring relies entirely on periodic surveys that are

years outdated by the time shocks occur. This study aims to develop aiPHeed, a geospatial food
insecurity forecasting system for DA Region IV-A using NLP and machine learning to support
anticipatory intervention and decision-making.

         Specifically, this study seeks to answer the following questions:
    1. What variables most significantly predict food insecurity risk across the provinces of

         CALABARZON, as identified through SHAP feature attribution?
    2. What is the furthest lead time at which aiPHeed can reliably forecast province-level food

         insecurity risk across CALABARZON?
    3. How does the LightGBM model combining NLP-derived news signals and government

         statistical indicators compare against six baseline approaches , naive persistence, three
         logistic regression variants (combined features, government indicators only, and NLP
         features only), and two LightGBM ablation variants trained on each data source
         separately , in classifying provincial food insecurity risk?
    4. How effectively do the city- and municipality-level food insecurity risk rankings derived
         from province-level forecasts reflect relative vulnerability across CALABARZON?

                                                                                                                             10
    5. How do DA Region IV-A personnel, as system evaluators, and DEPDev Region IV-A
         personnel, as usability evaluators, assess aiPHeed under the ISO/IEC 25010 software
         quality model, in terms of functional accuracy and ease of use respectively?

1.4 Hypothesis
         This study tests five null hypotheses, each addressing the relationship between an

independent variable and a dependent variable. The independent variables are the news-derived
signals and the government socioeconomic indicators. The dependent variable is the binary food
insecurity risk classification, either high or low, of a CALABARZON province in a given
quarter. The study's assumptions are based on the null hypothesis that these independent
variables do not carry predictive signals strong enough for aiPHeed to classify, forecast,
disaggregate, or be judged useful for that purpose.

         Specifically, the study sought to address the following:

    1. The Food Stress Sentiment Index (FSSI) and food CPI deviation will not be among the
         strongest predictors of food insecurity risk in CALABARZON when examined through
         SHAP feature attribution.

    2. aiPHeed will not reliably classify whether a CALABARZON province is at high or low
         food insecurity risk across multiple forward-looking lead times.

    3. The LightGBM model that combines news signals and government data will not
         outperform the six comparison models, naive persistence, three logistic regression
         variants (news-only, government data-only, and combined), and two LightGBM variants
         trained on only one data source at a time, in classifying provincial food insecurity risk
         across CALABARZON.

                                                                                                                             11
    4. The vulnerability-weighted spatial disaggregation of province-level forecasts will not
         produce municipal and city risk rankings that are consistent with DA Region IV-A
         evaluators' assessments of relative food insecurity vulnerability across CALABARZON,
         as evaluated through the aiPHeed Post-Survey Questionnaire.

    5. aiPHeed will not receive favorable functional suitability, accuracy, and usability ratings
         from DA Region IV-A evaluators under the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model.

1.6 Significance of the Study
         The development of aiPHeed carries significant implications for the following

stakeholders:
         DA Region IV-A. As the government agency directly mandated to promote agricultural

productivity and food security across CALABARZON, DA Region IV-A will review and gain
access to quarterly province-level food insecurity risk forecasts through a secure web dashboard,
supporting more proactive planning of agricultural interventions and allocation of resources.
SHAP-based explanations will make model predictions interpretable for decision-makers, while
the system may also serve as a model for integrating artificial intelligence into public service
delivery elsewhere in government.

         Local Government Units (LGUs). LGU social welfare officers across CALABARZON
will have access to spatially disaggregated municipal and city-level forecasts to support proactive
intervention and local planning. The system's localized outputs can help officials identify at-risk
communities earlier to improve disaster preparedness and resource mobilization efforts.

         Communities and Vulnerable Populations. Earlier identification of high-risk areas will
support faster and more targeted delivery of food assistance, nutritional support, and livelihood
programs. The system's spatial targeting approach may also help reduce resource misallocation,
particularly in vulnerable and geographically isolated communities across CALABARZON.

1.7 Scope and Limitations
         This study will develop and evaluate aiPHeed, a web-based system that forecasts food

insecurity risk for the 142 cities and municipalities in Region IV-A, covering Cavite, Laguna,
Batangas, Rizal, and Quezon. The system will provide an interactive map showing food

                                                                                                                             14
insecurity risk levels and rankings, together with the factors influencing each forecast. A
downloadable PDF assessment report containing forecast scores, SHAP driver breakdowns,
performance metrics, and data-sufficiency disclosures will be available for each province and
CALABARZON as a whole. The system will use Filipino and English news articles from 2020
to 2025 and publicly available data from PSA, DOST-FNRI, SWS, PAGASA, PhilRice, BSP, and
DOE, covering food prices, hunger, nutrition, agriculture, climate, and economic conditions.

         SWS quarterly hunger data and PSA food price data will be used to determine food
insecurity risk, while DOST-FNRI data will provide regional food security references. City and
municipal estimates will be derived from province-level forecasts using PSA Small Area
Estimates and population data; therefore, these estimates will not represent official food
insecurity classifications. Areas with limited news coverage will also be identified to disclose
insufficient information.

         The study is limited to Region IV-A and publicly available data. Restricted government
records, real-time field reports, and satellite-based data will not be included. The availability and
release schedules of government data may affect forecast timeliness, as publication delays may
postpone data collection, model updates, and report generation. The system will provide food
insecurity risk indicators only and will not replace official government assessments,
classifications, or policy decisions. Evaluation will involve 50 DA Region IV-A evaluators using
the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality standard.

                                                  CHAPTER II

                                REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE
         This chapter reviews existing literature and studies, both foreign and local, that are
relevant to the theoretical grounding, methodological design, and contextual understanding of the
proposed system. The works examined herein cover a range of topics, all of which collectively
inform the development of aiPHeed.

2.1 Foreign Literature
         The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations defines food security as

existing when all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient,
safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and
healthy life, operationalized through four interrelated dimensions: availability, access, utilization,
and stability. Ibnu (2025) integrated these dimensions into a national conceptual model and
strategic roadmap, arguing that institutional repositioning and predictive digital systems are
essential for affordability, equitable distribution, and resilience to disruptions. The study
proposes no operational forecasting tool or deployable system.

         International organizations have developed structured assessment frameworks that
informed the use of machine learning for food security forecasting. Westerveld et al. (2021)
demonstrated that machine learning models coupled with IPC transition data can forecast
monthly food security changes at livelihood-zone granularity with lead times of up to twelve
months, with predictions of deteriorations and improvements outperforming conventional
baselines and remaining transferable across geographic contexts. The study produced no
geospatial visualization interface and incorporated no NLP-derived signals.

                                                                                                                             16
         Among machine learning algorithms suited for structured prediction tasks, LightGBM
offers faster training speed and lower memory usage through its histogram-based splitting and
leaf-wise tree growth strategy. Indah et al. (2025) directly compared Random Forest, XGBoost,
and LightGBM on a structured socioeconomic classification task, finding that LightGBM
achieved superior predictive accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. The study
applied no temporal forecasting, NLP features, or geospatial output, making it a static
cross-sectional exercise.

         Composite index construction and geospatial visualization have also been applied to food
security monitoring at the community level. Pontin et al. (2023) constructed a multi-domain
Priority Places for Food Index synthesizing open data across seven domains, including healthy
food access, food bank demand, and deprivation indicators, paired with an interactive
geovisualization shaped through iterative engagement with policymakers to support reactive
monitoring and prioritization of intervention areas. The work included no machine learning
forecasting pipeline or NLP-derived leading indicators.

         The convergence of machine learning, remote sensing, and geospatial visualization has
produced international systems for food security prediction and agricultural risk modeling.
Preston and Basener (2023) integrated MODIS satellite imagery, demographic data, economic
indicators, climate variables, and supply-chain information into probabilistic machine learning
models estimating country-level food insecurity probabilities across Africa, demonstrating that
combining heterogeneous data sources substantially improves prediction reliability. The
approach is limited to the African context and excludes NLP signals.

                                                                                                                             17
2.2 Foreign Studies
         The global shift toward data-driven food security monitoring has accelerated since 2020,

driven by advances in machine learning, growing availability of secondary datasets, and the
recognition that periodic survey-based systems respond too slowly to emerging food stress
events. Martini et al. (2022) demonstrated in Nature Food that machine learning applied
exclusively to secondary data can reliably estimate sub-national food insecurity conditions, with
food inflation emerging as one of the strongest predictors and models explaining up to 81% of
variation in insufficient food consumption and 73% of crisis-level coping.

         Similarly, Foini et al. (2023) constructed XGBoost models trained on near real-time WFP
food consumption observations, introducing a walk-forward expanding-window retraining
scheme. Both studies, however, were limited to Africa and the Middle East, operated without
geospatial visualization, and made no use of NLP-derived signals.

         Examining forecast reliability across longer horizons, Busker et al. (2024) trained
separate XGBoost models for seven lead times spanning 0 to 12 months, capturing food security
dynamics up to a 3-month lead time with an R-squared above 0.6, performing comparably to
FEWS NET outlooks. Herteux et al. (2024) benchmarked five algorithm families for 60-day
ahead food consumption forecasts, finding gradient boosting remained competitive with more
computationally intensive deep learning architectures. Both studies validated forecasts only at
the first sub-national administrative level without municipal disaggregation and neither adapted
their frameworks for Southeast Asian data environments.

         Among the algorithms evaluated, Indah et al. (2025) directly compared Random Forest,
XGBoost, and LightGBM using 2023 Indonesian data, finding LightGBM achieved the highest
classification accuracy at 94.4%, attributed to its leaf-wise tree growth strategy, with per capita

                                                                                                                             18
expenditure identified as the most influential predictor. The study applied no temporal
forecasting, NLP features, or geospatial output, making it a static cross-sectional exercise.

         Beyond structured data, Balashankar et al. (2023) demonstrated in Science Advances that
a Random Forest classifier trained on 170 text features from 11.2 million news articles reduced
forecast RMSE by 34% over traditional baselines, with the combination of news and traditional
indicators achieving a 45% reduction and outperforming expert FEWS NET forecasts across all
16 countries. The study relied on English-language news only and required a supervised keyword
taxonomy with manual feature engineering.

2.3 Local Literature
         The Expanded National Nutrition Survey is the most extensive official research on

household food insecurity in the Philippines. The Department of Science and Technology
through the Food and Nutrition Research Institute (2024a) carried out a three-year rolling survey
from 2018 to 2021, covering all 81 provinces and 33 highly urbanized cities, using the Food
Insecurity Experience Scale to ascertain the level of difficulty families faced in accessing food.
Because the survey is cyclical, official data remains unchanged even as prices rise, typhoons
strike, and jobs are lost between survey cycles.

         Reporting on nutrition conditions across the Philippines, DOST-FNRI (2024b) found
mixed results: moderate-to-severe food insecurity showed marginal overall improvement, yet
severe food insecurity worsened among the highest-risk households, and economic recovery
from the 2022 price shock did not extend to the most vulnerable. These findings, presented a full
year after data collection, illustrate how far behind real time official reporting can run, and will
later serve alongside the 2021 ENNS as a validation baseline in Chapter III.

                                                                                                                             19
         The National Economic and Development Authority (2023) through the Philippine
Development Plan 2023 to 2028 commits to anticipatory and adaptive social protection,
acknowledging that Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program and Listahanan rely on household
assessments conducted only once every four to five years. The plan articulates this direction
clearly but provides no technique, example, or instance of how anticipatory tools should be
implemented.

         The Philippine Statistics Authority (2024) publishes monthly Price Situationer reports
covering prevailing prices for rice, corn, vegetables, fish, and meat across regions, including the
rice price crisis of 2023 and 2024. Price movements alone, however, do not capture how
households are affected, as the impact varies by socioeconomic context.

         The Philippine Statistics Authority Regional Statistical Services Office IV-A (2025)
produces the most detailed official poverty data for CALABARZON broken down by province
and municipality, showing poverty rates ranging from 6.7 percent in Cavite to 10.2 percent in
Quezon. Published only once every three years, it includes no quarterly risk breakdowns that
anticipatory programs require.

         PAGASA (2024) observes and evaluates the impact of rainfall patterns, droughts, floods,
and vegetation health on rice and corn production, but does not translate these into
household-level implications, which is critical in CALABARZON where typhoons hit Quezon
severely, Taal's activity affects Batangas, and irregular rainfall disrupts crops in Laguna and
Rizal.

         The Ateneo School of Government and SDSN Philippines (2024) compiled an anthology
of webinar reports from the SDSN Working Group on SDG2, representing perspectives from
policymakers, academics, agricultural scientists, and civil society practitioners on Philippine

                                                                                                                             20
food system and nutrition gaps. The collection identifies common problems but offers no
practical tools, though the need for more frequent and reliable information to fill the gap between
official survey timelines is clearly articulated throughout.

2.4 Local Studies
         The 2018­2021 ENNS rounds provide a longitudinal basis for identifying

household-level predictors of food insecurity in the Philippines. Semeniano et al. (2025) found
that vulnerability was associated with older household heads, larger families, renting a dwelling,
4Ps membership, and residing in historically food-insecure regions, while marriage, higher
education, greater wealth, rural residence, and home gardening reduced vulnerability. The
analysis is limited by a static time-series that cannot estimate changes over time or account for
price and climate shocks.

         Machine learning has also been applied to nutrition-related outcomes in the Philippines.
Siy Van et al. (2021) tested four algorithms on data from 618 students across one rural and one
urban site, finding Random Forest achieved the highest accuracy at 78.55% in identifying
children at nutritional risk. The study is limited to a single demographic subgroup and two
locations, with no forecasting component or broader geographic coverage.

         Parreño and Anter (2024) compared four machine learning models for forecasting rice
and corn yield using historical quarterly PSA data from 1987 to 2023, with Random Forest
obtaining the lowest error values across all three error metrics. The study carried out forecasts
only at the national aggregate level without provincial disaggregation, used no NLP-based
leading indicators, and produced no interactive geospatial visualization.

         At the sub-provincial level, machine learning has demonstrated strong predictive power
for poverty classification in the Philippines. Onsay and Rabajante (2025) applied it to

                                                                                                                             21
Community-Based Monitoring System datasets across 34 localities in the poorest region of
Luzon, where Random Forest regression achieved an R-squared of 0.92 and classification
achieved 95.94% accuracy. The study incorporated no NLP-derived features, produced no
geospatial visualization, and generated no forward-looking forecasts.

         Ledesma et al. (2020) developed an interpretable poverty estimation approach in the
Philippines using social media indicators, low-resolution satellite imagery, and volunteered
geographic information, demonstrating the feasibility of combining alternative data sources for
sub-national welfare estimation. The study's reliance on low-resolution inputs and black-box
deep learning models limits its operational deployment in resource-constrained government
settings.

         The literature reviewed above shows three gaps. Geographically and linguistically, the
systems with genuine predictive power, such as Westerveld et al. (2021), Martini et al. (2022),
Foini et al. (2023), and Balashankar et al. (2023), were built and validated in IPC-reporting
regions on English-language news, leaving a Filipino-English media environment and the Metro

                                                                                                                             25
Manila-versus-rural reporting imbalance in Philippine news coverage unaddressed. Technically,
the reviewed systems each cover at most one or two of the three components aiPHeed integrates,
NLP-derived indicators, machine learning forecasting, and a geospatial dashboard: forecasting
appears in Pontin et al. (2023) and Onsay & Rabajante (2025), geospatial disaggregation in
Parreño & Anter (2024) and Semeniano et al. (2025), and Filipino-English NLP in Preston &
Basener (2023), but not combined in one pipeline. Operationally, official Philippine monitoring,
ENNS, Listahanan, and PSA poverty statistics, is comprehensive but multi-year, and while the
Philippine Development Plan 2023­2028 calls for anticipatory, quarter-level tools, it does not
specify how to build one; the Philippine studies reviewed here, including Ledesma et al. (2020),
Siy Van et al. (2021), and Onsay and Rabajante (2025), forecast food insecurity at the regional
level rather than below it. aiPHeed responds to all three gaps within one deployable pipeline: it
applies zero-shot Filipino-English transfer learning to a Philippine news corpus for food
insecurity monitoring without annotated training data, derives a quarterly, province-level
CALABARZON forecast target from SWS hunger prevalence and PSA food CPI deviation
anchored to the FAO SDG 2.1.2 baseline given the continued absence of a public Philippine food
insecurity label, and pairs that forecast with bias correction for rural media undercoverage,
multi-lead-time LightGBM classification, SHAP explanation, and vulnerability-weighted
disaggregation to the municipal and city level, delivered through a public dashboard for DA
Region IV-A.

  RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

  This chapter outlines the research methodology, covering software development, data

gathering, preprocessing, algorithm design and its rules, and feature engineering. It further

presents the frameworks and process flow diagrams. These describe the approach employed in

the design, implementation, and evaluation of aiPHeed.

3.1 Software Development Methodology
Figure 3.1.1
aiPHeed Agile Software Development Methodology

         The aiPHeed system will be developed using an Agile methodology organized into a
continuous six-phase cycle: Plan, Design, Develop, Test, Deploy, and Review. This will ensure
that aiPHeed will evolve with each iteration as a continuously self-improving anticipatory
intelligence system rather than as a one-time deliverable.

                                                                                                                             27
         The Plan phase will consolidate the research direction. The researchers will define the
research direction by reviewing related food insecurity monitoring systems, formulating the
study objectives, identifying system requirements, and determining the primary data sources.
This phase will establish the core components of aiPHeed, including the Filipino-English NLP
pipeline, LightGBM forecasting model, and interactive geospatial dashboard.

         The Design phase will translate the plan into architecture. The researchers will design
data processing pipeline, Food Stress Sentiment Index (FSSI), feature matrix structure,
composite stress labeling method, spatial disaggregation process, SHAP interpretability
integration, and dashboard wireframes. MLOps configurations such as MLflow tracking and
APScheduler automation will also be prepared.

         During the Develop phase, the researchers will implement the system modules, including
geocoding, sentiment analysis, trigger classification, topic modeling, FSSI generation, feature
fusion, LightGBM forecasting, spatial prediction, and SHAP-based explanations. The FastAPI
backend and React-based dashboard will also be developed.

         The Test phase will validate system accuracy, reliability, and integration. Unit and
integration testing will verify individual modules and end-to-end workflows. The LightGBM
model will be evaluated using walk-forward cross-validation and compared against baseline
models using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.

         In the Deploy phase, the validated system will be deployed through a FastAPI backend
and React-Leaflet dashboard integrated with the DA Region IV-A review interface. APScheduler
will automate quarterly retraining and forecasting, while MLflow will manage model versioning
and experiment tracking.

                                                                                                                             28
         Finally, the Review phase will evaluate the deployed system through system accuracy
and usability assessments guided by the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model, conducted by
DA Region IV-A evaluators. Feedback gathered from this phase will guide future improvements
to the dashboard, forecasting thresholds, explainability features, and data integration, supporting
continuous system enhancement through succeeding Agile iterations.
3.2 Conceptual Framework
Figure 3.2.1
Conceptual Framework

                                                                                                                             29
         This framework will follow a six-stage sequential pipeline. Stage 1 will collect a
Filipino-English news corpus of 6,085 articles together with structured government indicators,
then preprocess the articles by removing HTML noise, merging the title and summary, truncating
the text to 250 characters, assigning each article to a quarter, and geocoding it to a
CALABARZON province through alias, substring, and fuzzy PSGC matching. Stage 2 will
apply XLM-RoBERTa in zero-shot mode to score every article against 10 bilingual hypotheses
adapted from the HungerGist taxonomy (Ahn et al., 2023), taking the highest entailment
probability among the five core food hypotheses as the article score. An article will be retained
as relevant only when this score is at least 0.30 and exceeds the best non-food hypothesis by at
least 0.20, and the relevant, province-level articles will be audited into a 371-article
CALABARZON set. Stage 2 will also classify stress drivers into five bilingual trigger
categories. Stage 3 will engineer features by correcting coverage bias, computing the FSSI with
its two lags and acceleration, computing the five trigger proportions, and deriving food CPI
deviation, unemployment rate, and rice price change, then merging these with the CPI-based
food stress label into a 135-row panel of 12 features covering five provinces across 27 quarters
(2020-Q1 to 2026-Q3).

         Stage 4 will train the risk classifier as a weighted blend of an Optuna-tuned LightGBM
model on all 12 features (weight 0.4), a logistic regression on the three government features
(weight 0.4), and a logistic regression on the climate, fish kill, and OFW remittance triggers
(weight 0.2), and a province-quarter will be classified HIGH when the blended score reaches
0.712. SHAP TreeExplainer will explain the LightGBM component, and province scores will be
disaggregated to 142 cities and municipalities using poverty and population density weights.
Stage 5 will validate the model through walk-forward expanding-window cross-validation and a

                                                                                                                             30
blind four-quarter holdout (2025-Q4 to 2026-Q3), route each province-quarter forecast through
DA administrator review, and measure system quality through an ISO/IEC 25010-based
questionnaire administered to 50 DA Region IV-A personnel. Stage 6 will report Accuracy,
Precision, Recall, F1-Score, AUC-ROC, and ISO/IEC 25010 quality scores as measures of
forecasting reliability and system quality.

3.3 Data Gathering and Pre-processing
         3.3.1 Data Gathering
                  The aiPHeed system will use two data types: a Filipino and English news corpus
         from publicly accessible online sources, and structured provincial statistical indicators
         from official Philippine government portals.

                   A Python-based article fetcher will collect Filipino-English news articles

                  deduplication, drawn from credible Philippine domains. Each record will be
                  stored with its title, link, publication timestamp, summary, source domain, and
                  unique identifier.

3.4 Algorithm and Its Rules
Algorithm 1: Zero-Shot XLM-RoBERTa Multilingual Sentiment Scoring

         Each article will be cleaned, concatenated, and truncated to 512 characters, then scored
by XLM-RoBERTa against ten bilingual HungerGist hypotheses (food supply disruption,
economic hardship, climate damage, displacement, OFW remittance loss). The maximum
entailment probability across all ten hypotheses will serve as the article's food insecurity score.
Articles scoring below 0.30 will be dropped. Qualifying scores will be averaged per
province-quarter, bias-corrected, and stored as the FSSI, from which lag and acceleration
features will be derived.

         The architecture of the proposed Zero-Shot XLM-RoBERTa Filipino-English Sentiment
Scoring contains:

                                                                                                                             37
     HTML cleaning and text preparation: HTML noise will be stripped from each
         article, the title and summary will be concatenated, and the resulting text will be
         truncated to 512 characters.

     Zero-shot scoring: The cleaned text will be passed through XLM-RoBERTa
         (joeddav/xlm-roberta-large-xnli) against each of the ten bilingual HungerGist
         hypotheses. The article's food insecurity score will be determined as the
         maximum entailment probability across all ten hypotheses.

     Score thresholding: Exclude articles scoring below 0.30 from aggregation to
         remove low-confidence signals.

     Bias-corrected FSSI aggregation: Average qualifying article scores per
         province-quarter and multiply by the spatial bias weight to produce FSSI(p,t)
         (fssi_builder.py).

     Lag feature construction: Derive FSSI_lag1, FSSI_lag2, and FSSI_accel for use in
         the LightGBM feature vector.

Components:
     Input Layer -- cleaned and truncated article text, max 512 characters per article
     XLM-RoBERTa Transformer Encoder -- will produce contextual embeddings
         that will be used for zero-shot classification via the
         joeddav/xlm-roberta-large-xnli checkpoint
     Output Layer -- continuous article-level food insecurity score that will be
         aggregated to province-quarter FSSI via bias-weighted mean
     Lag Feature Construction -- will compute FSSI_lag1, FSSI_lag2, and FSSI_accel
         for the LightGBM feature vector

Algorithm 2: Regex-Based Trigger Classification
         Each geocoded article will be matched against precompiled word-boundary regex

patterns across five bilingual categories: market, climate, employment, ofw_remittance, and
fish_kill. An article may trigger multiple categories simultaneously. Per province-quarter, the
proportion of articles triggering each category produces five continuous features (0­1) that will
enter the feature matrix and drive SHAP attribution narratives on the dashboard.

         The architecture of the proposed Regex-Based Trigger Classification contains:
              Taxonomy matching: Apply precompiled word-boundary regex patterns for each
                  of the five bilingual driver categories simultaneously to the combined article title
                  and summary.
              Multi-label assignment: Assign each article to all categories whose patterns
                  match. One article may trigger multiple categories.
                                                                                                                             39
     Proportion computation: For each province-quarter, divide the trigger count per
         category by the total article count, producing five continuous proportion scores
         bounded between zero and one.

     Feature output: Enter all five proportion scores as independent columns in the
         feature matrix and use them to populate SHAP attribution narratives in the
         dashboard.

Components:
     Input -- geocoded articles per province-quarter
     Bilingual Regex Matcher -- precompiled word-boundary patterns across five
         driver vocabularies in English and Filipino/Taglish
     Multi-label Assignment -- one article may trigger multiple categories
         simultaneously
     Output -- five continuous trigger proportion features per province-quarter,
         bounded between zero and one
